The thesis

Every other support course teaches the script. AI now writes the script.

Answering an order-status question, drafting a first-pass reply, finding the right help-centre article, summarising a long thread before a handover — these are the tasks a beginner support course spends most of its length on, and they are exactly the ones a chatbot or an AI copilot now handles adequately, for a few dollars a month, without a shift pattern. Teaching someone to be a fast, polite ticket-closer in 2026 is teaching them to compete with software on software's terms.

What a brand still pays a person for is narrower and much more defensible: the judgement call on what a complaint is really about, the discretion to handle a customer's sensitive situation without it becoming office gossip, the read on when a policy should bend and when holding the line is what's actually fair, and the relationship with the customer who has bought from this brand for two years and is quietly deciding whether to keep doing so. A chatbot cannot decide that a "where's my order" message is actually the fourth complaint from someone about to leave a one-star review and go quiet forever.

So this course teaches the channels and the tools in order to teach the judgement sitting on top of them — and it never teaches a conversation without teaching what to do when it goes wrong.

The sequence is built the same way Course 01's was: it front-loads the two skills every support job post actually tests — running a stacked ticket queue, and staying steady with an angry customer — because a learner who stops halfway has still learned the two things that get someone a trial shift. It puts getting paid at module 9 rather than as a footnote, for the same reason it mattered in Course 01: cross-border payment friction is what makes people quit remote work, not the work itself. And it treats the AI chatbot as a colleague to supervise from module 7 onward, not a threat to out-type — because the rep who reviews and corrects the bot is worth more than the rep the bot quietly replaces.

Module one, written in full

What a support rep is actually paid to keep

Five lessons, about fifty-five minutes. This is the free opener, so it has to do two jobs at once: teach something genuinely useful in the first ten minutes, and make the rest of the course feel necessary. The running example throughout modules 1–9 is Tolu Bankole, who runs support for Bumi Naturals — a Lagos skincare and wellness brand shipping across Nigeria and starting to ship to Ghana and Kenya.

1.111 min

Nobody hires a support rep to close tickets. They hire one to keep customers.

Here is the first thing to unlearn. You are not being paid to make a number go to zero. A queue at zero tells a founder nothing about whether the customer who wrote in is still going to buy again. Every brand that has ever hired a support person did it at a specific moment: the moment they noticed a customer had gone quiet after a bad experience, or a founder realised they were the one answering "where is my order" at midnight instead of sleeping.

That is the thing you are actually being paid for — the customer who was about to leave, staying instead. Ticket volume, response time, a tidy inbox: all mechanism. None of it is the job.

Why does this matter on day one? Because it changes how you answer. A rep who thinks in tickets closes the "where is my order" message with a tracking link and moves on. A rep who thinks in customers notices this is the third message from the same person this month, checks whether the pattern is a shipping problem worth flagging, and answers the tracking question and the anxiety underneath it — because a customer who has been ignored twice is not really asking about a parcel anymore.

One of those reps gets replaced by a chatbot within a year, because a chatbot can send a tracking link. The other becomes the person a founder cannot imagine running the brand without.

There is a version of this worth carrying with you: the ticket is the surface, the relationship is the job. A brand can survive a slow reply. It struggles to survive a customer who quietly decides, after one bad interaction, to never mention them to a friend.

  • Tickets versus customers, and the sentence that gives away which one you are managing
  • Why a repeat contact from the same person is worth more attention than a new one
  • The rep who gets replaced by a chatbot, and the one who does not — what actually separates them
Do this now

Think of the last time you personally contacted a company with a problem and it went badly. Write down, in one sentence, what would have made you stay a customer anyway. Keep it — you will use it in 1.5.

1.212 min

The four kinds of support work, and which two actually pay

Beginners lose their first months to the wrong kind of support work. Not to a lack of skill — to taking the first shift offered without noticing which of four very different jobs it actually is.

The overwhelmed founder's whole inbox. A solo skincare brand, a small SaaS tool, a one-person online shop — real revenue, no team, drowning in the same fifteen questions every day. Tolu Bankole's actual first client, Bumi Naturals, is exactly this. They can pay, they decide alone, and they feel the pain every single morning. Best client, and most beginners overlook them because the brand is small.

The small business with a growing queue. Five to twenty staff, one overworked person handling support alongside three other jobs. Slower to bring you on properly, but once you are trusted, the role grows for years.

The scaling startup. Funded, generous when things are going well, volatile when they are not. They will ask you to cover sales chat and onboarding too, and the role can vanish in a funding gap. Take them, but never make them your only client.

The ticket farm. Advertises "support agent needed, must handle 80+ chats a day, $2.50/hr, immediate start." This is not a client, it is a treadmill measured entirely on speed. Learn to recognise this post in ten seconds and close the tab — every hour spent here is an hour not spent becoming someone paid for judgement instead of throughput.

On rates: entry-level offshore support work commonly sits at the low end of what a task-based platform pays, while a rep trusted with a brand's whole queue and voice reaches considerably more, especially once retained monthly rather than paid per shift. The gap is rarely about typing speed. It is about whether the client believes they are buying replies or buying judgement.

  • Red flags in a support job post: "must handle 80+ chats", no pay named, "immediate start"

Do this now

Open any freelance or remote job board and read fifteen customer-support posts without applying to any. Sort them into the four types. Notice how many are ticket farms, and how differently the good ones are written.

1.313 min

What AI chatbots already do better than you, and what that leaves

This is the lesson other courses will not give you, because it makes their curriculum look thin. Better to hear it in the first hour than after a year of learning the wrong half of the job.

AI chatbots and copilots now competently answer routine, repeated questions — order status, return eligibility, how a product is used, opening hours, a first-pass reply to a simple complaint. Those capabilities are already deployed by companies of every size, cost little, and never need a shift covered. If your pitch is "I reply fast and I am polite", you are pricing yourself against something that costs a few dollars a month and answers in two seconds.

Now the other half, and it is the half that pays. There are things no bot does well, and they are not small: judgement about what a complaint is really about — a bot answers the tracking question; it does not notice this is the fourth message from someone about to churn. Discretion — someone has to handle a customer disclosing a health reason for a return, or a complaint that names an employee, and be trusted not to mishandle it. Reading when policy should bend — a bot applies the rule; a person notices when applying it exactly would be technically correct and obviously wrong. Relationships — a repeat customer wants to feel recognised, not processed by a script that has never met them before.

Here is the reframe the whole course rests on: you are not competing with the chatbot, you are supervising it. The rep who lets the bot handle routine volume, reviews what it sends before it goes wrong, and steps in for the conversations that need a person is worth more than the rep who answers everything by hand — and worth considerably more than a founder trying to run the bot alone, because the founder does not want to check its work either. Module 7 covers the mechanics of that supervision; this lesson is where you accept the shape of the job.

Do this now

Take five tickets from a real support job post or help centre and sort each into two columns: "a bot could handle this acceptably" and "this needs a person". Write one line on what would have to go wrong for a column-one ticket to suddenly need a person. That line is where your value lives.

1.410 min

The trust ladder: from a trial shift to owning the whole queue

Nobody hands a stranger their support inbox and their brand voice on day one. Understanding this saves you from the two mistakes that sink most beginners: asking to own the whole queue too early, and staying stuck on the bottom rung too long.

Rung one, the trial shift. A few hours, one channel, low stakes, easy to check. The test is not whether you can answer correctly. It is whether you flag the thing you were unsure about instead of guessing silently — Tolu's first trial shift for Bumi Naturals was three hours on email only, and the moment that got him hired was a single message: "I wasn't sure whether to offer a replacement or a refund on ticket #114, so I held it — here's what I'd lean toward and why."

Rung two, the recurring shift. A regular slot each week, the same responsibilities each time. The test is consistency — week four looks like week one, and the founder stops double-checking your replies.

Rung three, the standing responsibility. You own the whole queue across every channel. The test is whether tickets stop reaching the founder at all, and whether you tell them what you handled without making them read a transcript of everything.

Rung four, the retainer with judgement attached. A fixed monthly fee for owning support as a function, not a shift. The test most people never see coming: whether you bring things to the founder. "I've noticed the same complaint five times this month — I think it's a packaging problem, not a support problem." A rep who only responds stays on rung three forever.

Every rung is passed on reliability and judgement, never once on typing speed or tool knowledge — which is the same thing the pain research behind this course found: execution and communication are what get someone hired and kept, not a long list of software names.

1.59 min

Your service, in one sentence a founder can repeat to someone else

You are going to write one sentence, and you will use it on LinkedIn, in every outreach message, and in the first thirty seconds of a trial-shift interview. It has to survive being repeated by someone else — because your third and fourth client often come from your first two describing you to a friend, badly, from memory.

"I do customer support" does not survive that. It says nothing about who you help or what stops going wrong.

The shape is: I help [a specific kind of business] stop losing [a specific thing] by [running a defined part of their support].

Compare. "I'm a customer service rep with experience in email, chat and social." Now: "I help small skincare and beauty brands stop losing repeat customers to slow replies by running their email and WhatsApp inbox." The second is narrower — and narrower is why it works. A founder who hears it recognises their own inbox.

The fear here is universal and worth naming out loud: if I narrow it down, I will get less work. The opposite happens, almost every time. Nobody searches for a generalist. Every founder hiring for support is thinking about their own specific queue, and they hire whoever sounds like they have already solved it for someone like them. You can hold two of these sentences, no more — module 10 revisits this once you have real work to point at.

You do not need to have done the work yet to write the sentence. You need to have chosen who you are for.

Do this now

Write your sentence. Read it out loud to one person and ask them to repeat it back tomorrow without looking. If they cannot, it is not the sentence yet.

D~45 min

Module drill: read the queue, not the job title

Five real customer-support job posts are supplied — one from each client type in 1.2, plus a deliberately bad one. For each post, write four short answers: what is this brand actually protecting right now (a rating, a repeat customer, a refund cost); which of these tickets would a chatbot already handle acceptably; what is left that genuinely needs a person; and what you would charge, with your reasoning.

Then choose the one post you would apply to, and write one sentence on why each of the other four is a trap. Do it before checking the worked solution — the point is not to match the answers, it is to notice where your reasoning differed and why.

What this drill is testing

Not knowledge. Whether you can look at a job post and see the customer behind it — the same skill that later lets you notice a pattern a founder has not spotted yet.
