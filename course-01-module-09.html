Lesson 9.114 minutes

Hourly, retainer or package: the arithmetic behind each, and when to use which

Three ways to charge for the same work produce three very different months. Hourly billing, a monthly retainer, and a fixed package each shift the risk of "this took longer than expected" onto a different person, and each rewards a different kind of behaviour. Picking the wrong one for a given client isn't just a pricing preference — it can quietly make the work unprofitable no matter how good you are at it.

Every pricing model is a different answer to one question: who absorbs the cost when a task takes longer than either of you expected?

The three models, and who carries the risk in each

  1. Hourly. You're paid for time spent, however long it takes. The client carries the risk of a task running over; you carry the risk of being paid only for hours actually worked, which punishes you for getting faster and more efficient over time. Best for genuinely unpredictable work — new clients, project scoping, anything without an established pattern yet.
  2. Retainer. A fixed monthly fee for a defined scope of ongoing work — Adaeze's inbox, calendar and file management, say, capped at an agreed number of hours or a general scope description. You carry the risk of a heavier month; the client carries the risk of paying the same amount in a lighter one. Best once the relationship is established and the workload is reasonably steady, because it rewards you directly for the efficiency you've built.
  3. Package. A fixed price for a defined, bounded piece of work — building the folder structure from module 4, or the podcast research from module 6. You carry essentially all the risk of it taking longer than priced; the client gets total cost certainty. Best for one-off, scopeable projects with a clear finish line.

Worked example: the same relationship, three phases

How Adaeze's engagement actually moved through all three

Month 1 — hourly. Nobody yet knew how much ongoing work the practice actually needed. Hourly billing let both sides find out without either one guessing wrong and losing money on the guess.

Month 3 — package. The file restructuring from module 4 was a one-off, boundable project — quoted as a fixed package once the scope was clear from the hourly month's data.

Month 4 onward — retainer. With a steady weekly pattern established — inbox, calendar, files, meetings — a retainer at a fixed monthly rate, capped at 25 hours, gave Adaeze budget certainty and rewarded getting faster at the recurring work.

Notice the order: hourly first, while nobody has enough information to price anything else safely. Retainers and packages both depend on being able to estimate effort reasonably well — pricing a large piece of work into a fixed retainer or package before you actually know what it takes is how skilled people end up quietly working for far less than their real rate.

The arithmetic that decides when to switch

Track your actual hours against any retainer or package for the first two or three cycles. If you're consistently well under the hours the price assumed, you've priced generously and can renegotiate upward with real data behind you. If you're consistently over, the price was too low for the real scope — raise it, narrow the scope, or move that piece of work back to hourly until it's better understood. The number that matters is your real effective hourly rate once a retainer or package is divided by actual hours worked — not the number that felt right when you quoted it.

Never quote a retainer or package on a guess

If you don't have real hourly data for a piece of work yet, price it hourly until you do, even if a client would prefer a fixed number sooner. A confident-sounding guess that turns out too low is much harder to walk back later than a short honest period of "let's track this properly first."

Keep this
  • Hourly, retainer, and package each put the risk of overrun on a different person.
  • Start new, unpredictable work hourly. Move to retainer or package once there's real data.
  • Track actual hours against any fixed price for the first few cycles, and adjust based on the real number.
  • Never quote a fixed price on a guess — an underpriced retainer is very hard to walk back later.
Do this now — 8 minutes

Think of a piece of work you do, or would do, for a client. Decide which of the three models fits it right now, honestly — not which one sounds most professional, but which one matches how well you can currently predict the effort involved.

Lesson 9.213 minutes

What your hour actually costs you: data, power, transport, tax, unpaid admin

A rate that looks reasonable on paper can still lose money once every real cost of doing the work is counted, not just the obvious ones. Most new assistants price from a number that felt fair, or that a course or forum suggested, without ever adding up what an hour of their own work genuinely costs them to deliver. This lesson is that addition.

Your rate isn't what you'd like to earn. It's what covers every real cost of showing up, plus what you'd actually like to earn on top of that.

The costs that quietly don't make it into most rate calculations

  1. Data and connectivity. Mobile data or a home connection reliable enough to work on, which in many contexts is a genuine recurring cost, not a background assumption.
  2. Power. Where electricity supply is unreliable, this can mean a generator, fuel, an inverter, or a subscription to backup power — a real monthly cost of simply being able to work consistent hours.
  3. Transport. Even remote-first work often includes occasional in-person meetings, banking errands, or client visits — real cost, real time.
  4. Tax and any required registration. Whatever your actual tax and business-registration obligations are where you operate — these are real costs of operating legitimately, and the specifics are worth confirming with a local accountant or official guidance rather than guessing, since rules and thresholds change.
  5. Unpaid admin. The time spent invoicing, chasing payment, doing your own bookkeeping, and — not incidentally — working through this course. None of it is billable to any single client, and all of it is real time your rate needs to cover.

A worked calculation

Building up from real costs to a minimum viable rate — illustrative numbers, not a universal figure
Target take-homeStart from what you actually need to earn monthly, after every cost below is covered.
Fixed monthly costsData, power backup, a portion of transport, any subscriptions or tools used specifically for client work.
Tax and admin set-asideA percentage held back for tax obligations, based on your own actual situation — confirm the real figure rather than assuming one.
Billable hours, realisticallyNot 40 hours a week — a realistic number after unpaid admin, unbillable client-development time, and the gaps between engagements are accounted for. Often closer to 20–25 billable hours in a working week than the full total.
Minimum rate(Target take-home + fixed costs + tax set-aside) ÷ realistic billable hours per month. This is the floor, not the number to quote — build in margin above it.

The step most people skip is the realistic billable-hours line. Counting a full working week as billable hours dramatically understates your real minimum rate, because a large share of any working week — even a well-run one — goes to things no single client pays for directly.

Revisit this at least twice a year

Costs change — data prices shift, power costs rise, your own circumstances change. A rate calculated honestly a year ago may already be too low for today's real costs. Treat this calculation as a recurring check, not a one-time exercise done before your first client and then forgotten.

Undercharging is not a kindness to future clients

Pricing below what your real costs require doesn't make you more competitive in any way that lasts — it makes the work unsustainable, which eventually means worse service, burnout, or quietly resenting clients for a rate you set yourself. Clients are generally better served by an assistant charging a sustainable rate than one who's quietly underwater.

Keep this
  • Five real costs: data, power, transport, tax and registration, unpaid admin.
  • Count realistic billable hours, not a full working week — often 20–25 hours, not 40.
  • Confirm your actual tax and registration obligations directly rather than guessing at a figure.
  • Revisit the calculation at least twice a year as real costs change.
Do this now — 10 minutes

Rough out your own five costs from this lesson, even approximately. Divide your target monthly take-home, plus those costs, by a realistic number of billable hours — not 40. Compare it honestly to whatever rate you'd have quoted before doing this exercise.

Lesson 9.313 minutes

The one-page agreement and the deposit rule

Adaeze and a new assistant agree verbally on scope, rate, and start date over a phone call, both genuinely intending to honour it. Three weeks in, a disagreement surfaces about whether weekend availability was included. Nobody's lying — memories of a verbal conversation just genuinely diverge over time, and there's nothing written to settle it. A one-page agreement exists to prevent this exact, entirely common situation, not to prepare for a worst-case dispute.

A written agreement isn't about distrust. It's about making sure two honest people remember the same thing three months later.

What actually needs to be on the page

Not a legal contract requiring a lawyer to draft — a clear, plain-language page covering the handful of things most likely to cause a misunderstanding later:

  1. Scope. What's included, stated specifically enough to be checkable — "inbox and calendar management, up to 20 hours a week" rather than "general assistance."
  2. Rate and payment terms. The number, the currency, when invoices go out, and when payment is due after that.
  3. What happens outside scope. How additional work beyond the agreed scope is handled — a higher rate, a separate quote, or a conversation first.
  4. Confidentiality. A plain statement that client information stays confidential — module 7 and 8's disciplines, made explicit in writing rather than assumed.
  5. How either side ends the arrangement. Notice period, and what happens to any work in progress or the handover file from module 4 if the engagement ends.

The deposit rule

For any new client relationship, or any package-priced piece of work from lesson 9.1, request a deposit before starting — commonly a third to half of the total, though the exact figure is a judgement call based on the relationship and the size of the work. A deposit does two things at once: it protects you from doing unpaid work if the relationship falls through early, and it's a genuine filter — a client unwilling to pay any deposit at all is worth a second look before committing real time to the relationship.

A one-page agreement — opening section

Scope: Inbox triage, calendar management, and file organisation, up to 15 hours per week.

Rate: [rate] per hour, invoiced monthly, payable within 7 days of invoice.

Outside scope: Additional projects (e.g. research tasks, document creation beyond routine templates) quoted separately before starting.

Confidentiality: All client and practice information remains strictly confidential, during this engagement and after it ends.

Ending the arrangement: Either side may end with 2 weeks' written notice. A handover document will be provided covering all active items.

Deposit: [amount], due before work begins, credited against the first invoice.

Keep it plain, not intimidating

The goal is clarity both sides can actually read and remember, not a document dense with legal language that signals distrust before the relationship has even started. Plain sentences, specific numbers, one page. If a client's situation genuinely requires more formal legal protection — significant sums, complex intellectual property, anything unusual — that's worth a real lawyer's input, but the great majority of assistant-client relationships are well served by a clear, honest page like this one.

Send it before the first invoice, not after a problem

The agreement should exist before any real disagreement, not get drafted defensively once one has already happened. Sending it as a normal, expected part of starting any new engagement — rather than only when something feels risky — keeps it from ever reading as an accusation.

Keep this
  • Five parts: scope, rate and terms, what happens outside scope, confidentiality, how it ends.
  • Request a deposit on any new relationship or package — it protects you and filters for serious clients.
  • Plain language, one page — clarity, not legal intimidation.
  • Send it as a normal part of starting any engagement, before any disagreement, not after.
Do this now — 10 minutes

Draft your own one-page agreement's five sections, even in rough form, for a real or hypothetical client relationship. Notice which section was hardest to write specifically — that's usually the one your actual practice is least clear about yet.

Lesson 9.414 minutes

Getting paid across borders: rails, fees, FX, and surviving the long wait

Adaeze's London and New York consulting clients pay in pounds and dollars. Getting that money into a usable Naira balance involves a currency conversion, a transfer rail, and — the first time it happened, before the route was understood properly — a wait of close to two months for one particular payment to actually clear and settle, caused by a slow correspondent-banking chain on the client's international wire and a new-account verification hold on the receiving end. Nothing was wrong, exactly. Nobody had mapped the route in advance, so nobody knew to expect it.

The fee you're quoted is rarely the whole cost. The exchange rate spread and the wait both cost real money — one visibly, one by tying up cash you needed sooner.

Three things that determine what a cross-border payment actually costs

  1. The explicit fee. Whatever the platform or bank states directly — a flat charge, a percentage, or both. The easiest part to compare, because it's the part everyone shows you upfront.
  2. The exchange-rate spread. The gap between the real market exchange rate and the rate you're actually given. This is frequently the larger hidden cost, particularly with a traditional bank transfer, and it's worth checking against a real market rate rather than trusting a platform's rate is close to it by default.
  3. The settlement time. How long between the sender paying and the money being usable. A route that's cheap in fees but takes three weeks to clear has a real cost too — the cash isn't available, which matters enormously if it's needed for rent or a supplier payment in the meantime.

Why the wait happens, specifically

A slow international transfer isn't usually one single delay — it's often several, stacked: the sending bank routes the payment through one or more correspondent banks rather than directly, each one adding processing time and sometimes a further currency conversion; compliance checks run automatically on transfers above certain thresholds or from new relationships, adding days without any visible explanation; and a receiving platform or bank may hold funds from a new or unverified sender account until its own checks clear. None of these individually take two months — stacked together, on a first payment from a new client through an unfamiliar route, they realistically can.

What actually reduces this, based on what happened

What changed after the first payment's long wait

Verified the receiving account fully — identity documents, business details — before the next payment was due, rather than mid-transfer, since new-account holds were a real part of what delayed the first one.

Asked the London client to send via a dedicated transfer service built for exactly this route, rather than their bank's standard international wire — generally fewer correspondent-bank hops for a well-established transfer corridor.

Compared the platform's quoted exchange rate against the real market rate before accepting it, rather than assuming it was close.

Built a cash buffer sized around the longest wait experienced so far, so a slow payment doesn't create an immediate cash crunch even if it happens again.

Map the route before the first real payment, not during it

Before the relationship's first invoice, actually test the intended payment route with a small amount if possible, note how long it genuinely took and what it genuinely cost after both the fee and the spread, and only then treat that route as reliable for a client's real payments. What a platform advertises and what a specific route actually delivers, on a first attempt with a new account, are not always the same thing.

This is exactly what module 9's drill will ask you to prove

A payment route is not properly understood until it's been tested with a real, if small, transaction — not just read about or assumed reliable from a platform's marketing page. The drill at the end of this module asks for exactly that proof, for good reason.

Keep this
  • Real cost is fee, plus exchange-rate spread, plus settlement time — not just the visible fee.
  • A slow transfer is usually several stacked delays: correspondent banking, compliance checks, new-account holds.
  • Fully verifying your receiving account in advance removes one of the most common causes of a long first wait.
  • Test a payment route with a small real transaction before trusting it for a client's actual payments.
Do this now — 8 minutes

If you have or plan to have international clients, research two real payment routes available to you. Note the explicit fee, and actually check the exchange rate offered against a real market rate for the same day. The gap is often larger than expected.

Lesson 9.511 minutes

Tracking time and invoicing without losing your evening to it

Friday evening, invoice day: forty minutes spent trying to reconstruct which tasks happened when, across three clients, from memory and a scattering of half-remembered messages. This happens to almost everyone who starts billing hourly without a system, and it is entirely avoidable with a habit that costs seconds, not minutes, if it happens at the actual moment work happens rather than after the fact.

Time tracking done in the moment costs seconds. Time tracking reconstructed from memory costs an evening and is usually wrong anyway.

Log at the moment of switching tasks, not at the end of the day

The single habit that makes this easy: log the task and the time the moment you move from one thing to the next, not in a batch at day's end. A simple running note — task, start time, end time — takes five seconds to update when switching tasks and is essentially always accurate, because nothing has been forgotten yet. Reconstructing the same information hours or days later is slow and reliably inaccurate, because memory rounds and merges tasks in ways a live log never does.

What a usable log actually needs

Four columns, no more
DateWhich day, obviously — needed for both invoicing and your own pattern-spotting over time.
TaskSpecific enough to be a real line on an invoice — "inbox triage," "MedSupply renewal call," not "admin."
ClientEssential the moment you have more than one — a log without this column is unusable for split invoicing.
DurationCalculated from start and end time, not estimated after the fact.

Turning the log into an invoice is then almost automatic

With a clean running log, invoicing becomes filtering by client and date range, then summing duration by task category — a matter of minutes rather than reconstruction. This is the entire payoff of the habit: the tedious part happens in five-second increments spread invisibly through the week, instead of arriving all at once as a dreaded Friday-evening task.

Sending the invoice itself: three habits worth keeping

  1. Send on a fixed schedule — the same day every week or month, regardless of how busy things are. An invoice that goes out "whenever there's time" arrives later and later, and payment follows the same drift.
  2. Make line items specific, mirroring the log itself, so a client can see exactly what they're paying for without needing to ask — this is also a natural extension of the accountable-record habit from module 5.
  3. Note payment terms on every invoice, even for a client you've worked with for years — "due within 7 days" stated plainly is a small habit that quietly shortens how long invoices sit unpaid.
A messy time log is a warning sign worth reading

If keeping a live log consistently feels like too much friction, that's often a sign the underlying rate model is wrong for the work — lesson 9.1's point about matching the model to the work applies here too. A retainer or package removes the need for granular time tracking almost entirely, at the cost of the risk-sharing trade-off covered in that lesson.

Keep this
  • Log at the moment of switching tasks — five seconds, always accurate. Never reconstruct from memory later.
  • Four columns: date, task, client, duration. Nothing more is needed.
  • Send invoices on a fixed schedule, with specific line items and stated payment terms every time.
  • Constant friction with time tracking is a sign the pricing model itself may be the wrong fit.
Do this now — 6 minutes

Set up the four-column log now, even if it's empty, in whatever tool you'd actually use day to day. The habit only works if the log already exists the next time you switch tasks — build it before you need it, not after the first messy Friday.

Module 9 drill75 minutes

Rate card, agreement, live payment route

This drill produces three real pieces of your own business infrastructure — not exercises about a fictional client this time, but the actual documents and a tested payment route you would use.

Deliver three things

  1. Your rate card. Built from your real costs using lesson 9.2's calculation — data, power, transport, tax set-aside, realistic billable hours — showing the arithmetic, not just a final number.
  2. Your one-page agreement. All five sections from lesson 9.3, written for a real or realistic client situation, in language you'd actually be comfortable sending.
  3. A tested payment route. Set up one real cross-border or local payment route end to end and prove it with a live test transaction — even a small one — noting the actual fee, the actual exchange rate against the real market rate, and the actual time it took to settle. A screenshot or description alone does not count; it needs to have actually moved.

How it is marked

  • Does the rate card show real arithmetic, not a round number picked because it felt right? Lesson 9.2's whole point was replacing a gut-feel number with one built from actual costs — a rate card with no visible working has skipped the actual lesson.
  • Does the agreement cover all five sections, specifically? A scope described as "general assistance" or confidentiality left unstated both fail the specificity this lesson asked for.
  • Was the payment route actually tested, with a real transaction? This is the one item in the whole course that cannot be faked with a description — lesson 9.4 was explicit that a route isn't understood until it's been tried for real.
  • Is the real cost of the payment route reported honestly — fee, spread, and time — not just the fee? A report that only mentions the advertised fee has missed the hidden costs lesson 9.4 spent most of its time on.
  • Would you actually be comfortable sending all three to a real client tomorrow? The test for whether this drill produced something real rather than a classroom exercise.
What this drill is really testing

Whether the business side of this role is actually built, not just understood in the abstract. Every previous module's drill tested a skill on Adaeze's behalf. This one turns the same discipline on your own business, because the course only pays off if the person who took it can actually get paid, reliably, for doing everything the first eight modules taught.

Before module 10

You should now have: a rate card built from real numbers, a one-page agreement you could send today, and a proven payment route with a real transaction behind it. Module 10 turns outward — choosing a niche, building proof of work, and finding and winning your first three real clients.
