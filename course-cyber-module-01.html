Lesson 1.19 minutes

Nobody is "too small to hack"

The most common thing people say after an account is taken over is: "Why me? I have nothing worth stealing."

That sentence misunderstands how attacks work. Most attackers are not choosing you. They run the same message or the same stolen password list against thousands of people at once and see who answers. You were not picked. You were reachable.

You are not hidden because you are small. You are simply cheap to try.

What "worth stealing" really means

Your phone number is the key to your mobile money wallet, your WhatsApp and every account that texts you a code. Your email inbox is the reset button for everything else you own. Your contacts are a ready-made list of people who will trust a message from you.

So the real question is never "do I have money?" It is "what can someone do while pretending to be me?"

Try it

Open your email and search for "password reset". Count how many different services have you on file. That is the size of the door that one email account holds shut.

Keep this
  • Attackers cast wide nets; being small is not protection.
  • Your email and phone number are the master keys to everything else.
Lesson 1.211 minutes

The attacker's economy: what your accounts are worth

Attacks are a business with costs and profits. Once you see the numbers the attacker sees, the advice in later modules stops feeling like a list of chores and starts feeling like common sense.

An attacker wants the cheapest route to something they can turn into money or leverage. That can be a direct cash-out (a mobile money transfer), a resale of the account itself, a scam that uses your name to reach friends and family, or access to your employer through you.

Why the cheap route is always people

Breaking strong technical protection takes skill and time. Persuading one tired person to read out a code takes a good story and a phone call. When the second option works, nobody bothers with the first. This is why most of this course is about habits, not software.

Security is mostly about making yourself a more expensive target than the next person.

Try it

Write down three things you would hate to lose access to tomorrow morning. For each, note which email address or phone number recovers it.

Keep this
  • Attackers follow the cheapest route to money or leverage.
  • The cheapest route is almost always a person, not a machine.
Lesson 1.312 minutes

The five ways in

Almost every personal or small-business incident fits one of five doors. You will meet each in detail later. For now, learn to name them.

1. Tricked. A message, call or page persuades you to hand over a password, code or payment (phishing and social engineering).

2. Reused. A password you used on a small site leaks, and attackers try it on your email and bank.

3. Unpatched. A phone, laptop or app with a known flaw and a pending update you keep postponing.

4. Exposed. A device or account with no screen lock, no second step, or a shared login many people know.

5. Inside. A lost phone, a former colleague who still has access, or a careless sharing setting.

Name the door, and you know which habit closes it.

Try it

Next to each of your three things from the last lesson, write the door you think is easiest to use against it.

Keep this
  • Five doors: tricked, reused, unpatched, exposed, inside.
  • Most incidents are the first two.
Lesson 1.412 minutes

Your personal threat model in fifteen minutes

A threat model sounds technical. It is three questions: what do I have, who might want it or misuse it, and what happens if it goes wrong?

A student might worry about a stolen phone and a hijacked WhatsApp. A freelancer might worry about client files and invoice fraud. A small shop owner might worry about mobile money and a shared till phone. Same method, different answers.

Rank, do not panic

List what you have, then score each item for how bad the loss would be and how likely it is. Fix the high-and-likely items first. You will not close every door in a week, and you do not need to.

Try it

Make a table with three columns: What I have, What would hurt, First fix. Fill in at least five rows. You will use this table again in Modules 2 to 5 and in the capstone.

Keep this
  • Threat model = what I have, who wants it, what happens if it goes wrong.
  • Fix high-impact, high-likelihood items first.
Module 1 drillabout 40 minutes

Map your own digital life

Build your threat model table and answer the questions below in one short page. Use real examples, but do not write any password or code in your answers.

  1. List your ten most important accounts and apps (email, WhatsApp, mobile money, bank, social, school or work).
  2. For each, write which email address or phone number recovers it. Circle any that depend on the same one.
  3. Pick the door (tricked, reused, unpatched, exposed, inside) most likely to be used against each.
  4. Choose your top three fixes and say what you will do this week.

Use the "Submit drill answer" bar at the bottom of the page to save your answers to your account. You can edit and resubmit later.
