The Assistant They Can't Replace / Module 06

Research and data you can be trusted with

Five lessons, just over an hour. "Can you look into this for me" is the most common request in this whole job, and the least defined. This module is how you turn a vague ask into a research question with an edge to it, know where to look and when to stop, verify what you find instead of trusting the first result, and hand it back as something Adaeze can act on in five lines.

Lesson 6.114 minutes

Turning a vague request into a research question with a stopping point

Adaeze messages: "Can you find me some podcasts I could be a guest on? Trying to get the practice's name out there a bit more." That is a real request and a completely unresearchable one, in this form. "Some" is not a number. "Get the name out there" is not a criterion anything can be checked against. Handed straight to a search box, this produces an hour of open-ended browsing that ends only when you get tired, not when the question is answered.

A research task without a stopping point doesn't end when it's finished. It ends when you give up.

Two things every vague request is missing

Almost every request that arrives shaped like Adaeze's is missing the same two things, and your job before you open a single tab is to supply them:

  1. Criteria — what makes something count. Not every podcast, obviously. Which ones? This is where you turn "get the name out there" into something checkable: an audience that would plausibly include her ideal clients, a format that takes guests (not solo-hosted), and a minimum sign of being active — an episode in the last two months, say, not a show that stopped recording in 2023.
  2. A stopping point — how you'll know you're done. A number ("thirty qualifying shows"), a time box ("three hours, whatever's found by then"), or a saturation point ("stop when the same five shows keep reappearing across every search"). Without one of these, "find some podcasts" has no finish line, and you will either under-deliver by stopping too early or burn an afternoon chasing diminishing returns.

Turning the request into a brief — before you search anything

Write the brief first, as a short exchange with Adaeze if you can, or as your own working assumption if you can't reach her quickly. Five minutes here saves an hour of research aimed at the wrong target.

The vague ask, turned into a brief

Original ask: "Find me some podcasts I could be a guest on."

Research question: Which active, guest-accepting podcasts have an audience likely to include people seeking a therapist — mental health, wellness, women's health, or Nigerian-diaspora-focused shows — that Adaeze could realistically pitch herself to as a guest?

Criteria to count: Takes outside guests (not solo format). At least one episode in the last two months. English-language. Audience plausibly includes people who'd consider therapy — inferred from topic and past guest list, not just the show's stated category.

Stopping point: Thirty qualifying shows, or four hours of research, whichever comes first — with a note on which limit was hit.

Notice the stopping point names both limits and says which one to report against. That single addition is what stops "I ran out of time" and "I found everything there is" from looking identical in the final handover — they are very different situations, and Adaeze needs to know which one she's getting.

When you can't ask before you start

Sometimes there's no time to clarify — the request arrives ten minutes before Adaeze goes into a session and you have to start now. In that case, write your own assumed criteria and stopping point at the top of your notes before searching a single thing, and flag them in the handover: "I assumed you meant shows with a wellness or mental-health-adjacent audience, and stopped at thirty — let me know if I should widen it." An assumption stated plainly is a fast, defensible substitute for a conversation you didn't have time for. A silent one is a guess dressed up as a fact.

Why this lesson comes before any actual searching

Every other lesson in this module — where to look, how to verify, how to present — only works once the target is this specific. Skipping this step and going straight to search is the single most common way research tasks in this job go wrong: not from bad searching, but from searching hard for the wrong thing.

Keep this
  • Every vague request is missing criteria and a stopping point. Supply both before searching anything.
  • A stopping point is a number, a time box, or a saturation signal — never "until it feels done."
  • When you can't ask first, write your own assumptions down and flag them in the handover.
  • Report which limit you hit — running out of time and running out of results are different situations.
Do this now — 10 minutes

Take a vague request you've received recently — from anyone, about anything. Write the research-question version: one sentence, plus criteria, plus a stopping point. If you can't write a stopping point, that's usually a sign the criteria aren't specific enough yet either.

Lesson 6.213 minutes

Where to look, and how to know when you have enough

With the brief from 6.1 written, the temptation is to open a search engine and start typing "mental health podcasts." That will work, eventually, but it's the slowest possible route — one search engine, one ranking algorithm, one kind of result. Good research fans out across a small number of genuinely different sources, because each one surfaces things the others miss.

Three kinds of source, not three searches

For almost any research task in this role, three different kinds of source will find things a single search never will:

  1. Purpose-built directories. For podcasts specifically: Apple Podcasts and Spotify's own category browsing, and directory sites built for exactly this (Listen Notes, Podchaser) that let you filter by category and sort by recency — built for the question you're asking, not a general search engine's best guess at it.
  2. Adjacent examples, worked backwards. Find two or three therapists or wellness practitioners who already do this well — who's been a guest on shows in this space recently? Their guest history is a shortcut to a whole cluster of relevant shows you'd otherwise find one at a time.
  3. The network inside what you've already found. Once you have five or six solid shows, look at who else has guested on them, what other shows their hosts mention or cross-promote, what similar shows their listeners are pointed toward. Relevant shows cluster; the first few you find are usually connected to several more.

The saturation test: how you actually know when to stop

Lesson 6.1 gave three possible stopping points — a number, a time box, or saturation. Saturation is the one that takes judgement, so it's worth spelling out: keep a running log of every show you find, and once new searches start returning shows you've already logged, more often than turning up new ones, you have found most of what's genuinely findable through this method. Continuing past that point burns time for very little new material — better to note that you've hit saturation and stop there, even short of a target number, than to keep searching out of a feeling that you haven't tried hard enough.

A source log is not optional

Keep a running note of every search term you tried and every source you checked, even the ones that turned up nothing useful. This does two things: it stops you accidentally repeating the same search under a different phrasing, and it lets you tell Adaeze exactly where you looked — which matters more than it sounds like it should, because "I checked Apple Podcasts, Listen Notes, and the guest lists of four comparable therapists" is a very different, and far more trustworthy, sentence than "I googled around."

Knowing when a source is worth trusting at all

Not every source deserves equal weight. A directory that's actively maintained and lets you filter by real signals (recency, episode count) is worth more than a "top 50 podcasts" listicle from a marketing blog with no visible update date — those lists are frequently years stale and copied from each other, which is exactly the kind of thing lesson 6.3's verification step exists to catch. Treat listicles as a starting point for names to check, never as a finished answer.

Keep this
  • Fan out across purpose-built directories, adjacent examples worked backwards, and the network inside what you've already found.
  • Log every search and source, even the ones that found nothing — it prevents repeated work and makes your method reportable.
  • Saturation — new searches mostly return what you've already logged — is a legitimate reason to stop, even short of a target.
  • Treat undated "top X" listicles as a lead list to verify, not a finished answer.
Do this now — 10 minutes

Pick any topic and try all three source types from this lesson — a purpose-built directory, one adjacent example worked backwards, and the network around your first few finds. Notice which one surfaced things the others didn't.

Lesson 6.314 minutes

Verification, and labelling how confident you are in each row

A directory listing says a podcast has "50,000 monthly listeners." The listing is two years old. The show's last episode was eight months ago. Do you include it in the thirty? This is the question verification exists to answer — and the honest answer is often not yes or no, but a label: include it, clearly marked as uncertain.

The failure mode in research is not usually including something false. It's presenting something uncertain as if it were confirmed.

Three confidence levels, and what earns each one

Rather than a binary in-or-out decision, sort every finding into one of three confidence levels. This is the single habit that makes research trustworthy rather than merely thorough:

Confidence labels
VerifiedConfirmed directly — you found a recent episode, checked the guest-pitch page yourself, or the host explicitly states they take outside guests. You'd stake your name on this row.
LikelyA reasonable inference from a real pattern — the show has featured guests before, on a related topic, but you didn't confirm they're currently accepting pitches. Probably right; not checked directly.
UnverifiedOne weak signal only — appeared on a single undated listicle, no direct confirmation of anything. Include only if it's genuinely promising and say plainly that it's unconfirmed.

What "verify" actually means in practice

For the podcast brief, verifying a single row means checking three things directly, not trusting the directory's word for any of them: does the show visibly take outside guests (an episode with a named guest, not just the host talking), is it still active (something published within the stopping point's recency criterion — two months, per the 6.1 brief), and is there a real way to pitch it (a contact email, a submission form, a booking platform listed) rather than just a general "contact us" with no clear route to guest pitches.

The same podcast, checked properly

Directory says: "Wellness Weekly Naija — mental health podcast, guest interviews, large following."

Checked directly: Most recent episode was 11 months ago — fails the two-month activity criterion. The last five episodes before that were all solo commentary, no guests. Listed "guest interviews" appears to describe a format the show used two years ago, not its current one.

Verdict: Excluded, with a one-line note in the source log — "inactive and appears to have dropped the guest format; found via listicle, not confirmed independently." Worth keeping the note in case the show restarts later.

Notice that excluding it is itself a finding worth recording, not just a row deleted silently. If Adaeze later asks "what about Wellness Weekly, I've heard of that one," the log already has the answer.

When you can't verify something but it's too promising to drop

Sometimes a show looks exactly right but you genuinely can't confirm activity or a guest format — the website is thin, there's no recent update anywhere. Don't quietly drop it, and don't quietly upgrade it to "likely" because you want it to be true. Include it labelled unverified, with the one specific thing that would confirm it: "unverified — promising audience fit, but no episode found in the last six months; worth a direct email to check if it's still active."

Why labels beat a strict cutoff

A strict rule — only include fully verified rows — sounds safer, but it silently throws away real leads that just happened to be harder to confirm in the time available. Labelling confidence instead keeps everything visible and lets Adaeze, who knows her own risk tolerance, decide how to use the "unverified" rows rather than never seeing them at all.

Keep this
  • Three labels — verified, likely, unverified — on every row, not a binary include/exclude.
  • Verifying means checking activity, guest format, and a real pitch route directly, not trusting the directory's description.
  • Record exclusions too, with the reason — it answers the question before it's asked again later.
  • Don't quietly upgrade an unverified finding to look more confirmed than it is, however promising it seems.
Do this now — 10 minutes

Take three claims from anywhere online — a stat, a fact, a listing — and actually check each one against a second, independent source. Label each verified, likely, or unverified based on what you find. Notice how often the honest label isn't "verified."

Lesson 6.412 minutes

Getting it into a spreadsheet the client can actually use

Thirty rows of research, properly verified and labelled, still fail if they land in Adaeze's inbox as a wall of text or a spreadsheet with fourteen unlabelled columns she has to decode before she can use any of it. The spreadsheet is not a storage format for what you found — it's the interface Adaeze will actually act through, so it needs to be designed for her, not for you.

A research spreadsheet succeeds when the client can sort, filter, and act on it without asking you what any column means.

Columns that earn their place

More columns is not more useful — it's more to read before doing anything. For the podcast brief, six columns carry the whole job:

The podcast research sheet — column by column
Show nameThe one thing every other column hangs off. First column, always.
ConfidenceVerified / Likely / Unverified, from lesson 6.3 — as its own filterable column, not buried in a notes field.
Audience fitOne short phrase — "wellness, Nigerian diaspora" — not a paragraph. Scannable at a glance down the whole column.
How to pitchThe actual contact route: an email, a form link, a named platform. The single most useful column for whether Adaeze can act today.
Last activeDate of most recent episode. Lets Adaeze sort by recency herself rather than trusting your activity judgement blindly.
NotesOne line, only when there's something genuinely worth flagging — not a running commentary on every row.

Sort and filter before you send it, not after

Set the sheet up sorted by confidence first, then by audience fit — so the thirty best bets are at the top, not scattered through the sheet in the order you happened to find them. Add basic filters on the confidence and audience-fit columns so Adaeze can narrow the list herself in ten seconds, rather than scrolling thirty rows every time she wants a subset.

One header row Adaeze needs before the table

Above the data, one short block: what the sheet covers, the criteria used to include or exclude a show, and the stopping point that was hit — the same three things from the brief in 6.1, now reported back. This is what turns a data dump into an accountable piece of work: anyone opening the sheet cold, months later, can see exactly what was searched for and why, without needing to ask you.

Resist the urge to add "just in case" columns

It's tempting to add a column for every fact you happened to collect — follower counts, episode length, host's other credentials. Unless a column changes what Adaeze does next, it's noise competing for attention with the columns that matter. If a fact is genuinely interesting but not decision-relevant, it belongs in the notes column, in one line, not its own column down the whole sheet.

Keep this
  • Six columns for the podcast brief: show name, confidence, audience fit, how to pitch, last active, notes.
  • Sort by confidence then relevance before sending — don't leave the client to sort thirty rows herself.
  • Add filters on the columns she'll actually want to narrow by.
  • One header block reporting criteria and stopping point, so the sheet is accountable on its own.
Do this now — 8 minutes

Open any spreadsheet you've built or received recently. Count the columns. For each one, ask whether it would change what someone does next. Cross out the ones that wouldn't — that's usually two or three fewer columns than you started with.

Lesson 6.510 minutes

Presenting a finding in five lines

The spreadsheet is done. Now comes the part most people skip: the message that goes with it. Send the sheet alone, with no summary, and Adaeze has to open thirty rows cold to figure out what she's looking at and whether it was worth the four hours. A five-line summary is what turns a finished spreadsheet into something she can act on in the ninety seconds she has between clients.

If you can't summarise a research task in five lines, you haven't finished thinking about what you found — you've only finished finding it.

The five lines, in order

  1. The headline finding. The single most useful sentence, stated as a fact, not a description of the process. "Found 27 active, guest-accepting podcasts with a wellness or diaspora-focused audience" — not "I did some research on podcasts."
  2. Method, in one line. Where you looked and what counted, briefly — enough for Adaeze to trust the number without reading the sheet. "Checked podcast directories, guest lists of comparable therapists, and their networks; counted a show if guest-accepting and active in the last two months."
  3. The top recommendation. Not the whole list — the two or three you'd actually start with, and one line on why. This is the highest-value sentence in the whole summary, because it saves Adaeze from having to do the final judgement call herself.
  4. The caveat. What's uncertain, incomplete, or worth treating carefully — the honest limit of the work. "Eight of the twenty-seven are labelled 'likely' rather than confirmed — worth a quick email before pitching those."
  5. The next step. What happens now, and whose move it is. "Sheet is sorted by confidence — let me know if you'd like me to draft pitch emails for the top five."

Worked example

The podcast research — five lines, sent with the sheet

Found 27 active, guest-accepting podcasts with an audience likely to include potential clients — mostly wellness, mental health, and Nigerian-diaspora-focused shows.

Checked three podcast directories, the guest lists of four comparable therapists, and the shows connected to those; counted a show if it takes outside guests and has posted an episode in the last two months.

I'd start with Mind & Motherhood and The Naija Wellness Hour — both actively feature therapists as guests and have a clear pitch route listed.

Eight of the 27 are labelled "likely" rather than confirmed — the shows look right but I couldn't verify current guest policy directly; worth a quick check before pitching those.

Sheet's attached, sorted by confidence. Happy to draft the first few pitch emails if useful.

Five lines is a discipline, not a hard limit

The number isn't sacred — it's a forcing function. Aiming for five lines is what stops the summary sprawling into a second document that restates the whole sheet. If a genuinely important sixth thing needs saying, say it — but notice that the temptation is almost always to add detail that belongs in the sheet's notes column, not new information Adaeze actually needs to act.

Send the summary in the message, not as an attachment

The five lines belong directly in the email or chat message, visible without opening anything. The sheet is the reference; the summary is what gets read. Burying the summary inside the spreadsheet's own header defeats the purpose — it should be the first thing Adaeze sees, before she's decided whether the attachment is worth opening at all.

Keep this
  • Five lines: headline finding, method, top recommendation, caveat, next step.
  • The recommendation line is the highest-value sentence — it does the final judgement call for the client.
  • State the caveat honestly. A summary with no caveat usually means one wasn't looked for.
  • Summary goes in the message itself, not buried inside the attachment.
Do this now — 8 minutes

Take any piece of research or reading you've done recently — for this course, for anything. Write the five-line summary now, cold, without looking back at your notes. Whatever you can't remember without checking is a sign of what wasn't actually the headline.

Module 6 drill65 minutes

Thirty podcasts, one sheet, five lines

You receive the exact request that opened this module: "Find me thirty podcasts I should be a guest on." Nothing else — no criteria, no deadline, no context beyond what you already know about Adaeze's practice from the last five modules.

Deliver three things

  1. The brief. Your own written research question, criteria, and stopping point, from lesson 6.1 — produced before any searching, and handed in alongside the rest so the reasoning is checkable.
  2. The sheet. Thirty rows (or fewer, with a clear note on which stopping-point limit was hit), using the six-column structure from lesson 6.4, sorted by confidence, with every row labelled verified, likely, or unverified.
  3. The five-line summary. Sent as it would actually be sent — in the message, not buried in the sheet.

How it is marked

  • Did the brief come before the searching? A brief written to justify results you'd already found, after the fact, reads differently from one written first — and it shows in how well the criteria actually match the final sheet.
  • Are the confidence labels honest? A sheet where everything is marked "verified" almost certainly wasn't actually checked row by row — this is the single easiest way to see whether lesson 6.3's discipline was really applied or just claimed.
  • Would Adaeze know how to pitch at least twenty of the thirty rows? The "how to pitch" column is the one that turns research into action; rows with no real contact route are a common way this drill quietly fails without anyone noticing.
  • Does the five-line summary actually save her from opening the sheet? Read the summary alone, without the sheet. If it doesn't tell her enough to make a first decision, it hasn't done its job.
  • Was the stopping point respected? Thirty-one hours of research producing forty rows fails the brief as clearly as stopping at twelve rows after twenty minutes does — the discipline from 6.1 is the point of this drill as much as the research itself.
What this drill is really testing

Whether you can take an underspecified request and impose enough structure on it — a real question, real limits, real verification — that the result is something a client can trust and act on without redoing your work herself. Every module's drill has tested judgement under a different kind of mess. This one is the mess of not knowing, which is the hardest kind to manage honestly.
