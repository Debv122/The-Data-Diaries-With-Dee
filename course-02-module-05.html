Refunds, returns and judgement under policy

Lesson 5.113 minutes

Reading a policy like a lawyer reads a contract

Most support reps read a returns policy once, remember the gist, and answer from memory after that. This is how "30 days" quietly becomes "about a month" in someone's head, and how a customer at day 33 gets refused for something the actual policy might have allowed under a clause nobody reread.

A policy is not a vibe you remember. It's a document with edges, and the edges are where the real questions live.

What to actually look for on a first careful read

Read for the exact numbers (is it 30 days from purchase, or from delivery — these are different dates and the gap between them matters at the edge). Read for named exceptions (some brands' policies exclude sale items, or make an exception for a manufacturing defect regardless of the window). And read for what the policy doesn't say — a genuine gap, which is different from an unfavourable answer, and is exactly where lesson 5.3's escalation judgement applies.

Worked example: the clause that changes the answer

Bumi Naturals' actual return policy, one clause

"Items may be returned within 30 days of delivery, except sale items, which are final. Products with a manufacturing defect may be returned at any time within 6 months, regardless of sale status."

A sale item, bought 40 days ago, with a broken pump: reads as "final sale, too late" on a fast skim. Read properly, it's a defect claim, not a change-of-mind return — a completely different clause, with a 6-month window, applies.

Keep this
  • Read the exact numbers and what date they count from — memory rounds these off dangerously.
  • Read for named exceptions, in both directions — some narrow the policy, some widen it.
  • A genuine gap in the policy is different from an answer you don't like — gaps get escalated, not guessed.
Do this now — 8 minutes

Find a real return policy — any brand's, publicly posted — and write down its exact window, what date it counts from, and any exception clause you find.

Lesson 5.212 minutes

The refund conversation: verifying without accusing

Some refund requests need a genuine check — a photo of the damage, an order number that matches, a claim that seems inconsistent with the shipping record. Asking for that verification is completely reasonable. Asking for it in a way that implies the customer is lying is how a legitimate request turns into a lost customer, even when the refund is eventually approved.

The shape of a verification request that doesn't accuse

Frame the ask as routine process, not suspicion of this specific person. "Could you send a quick photo of the damage? — it's just so I can flag the batch to our supplier" does real verification work while giving the customer a reason that isn't "I don't believe you."

Same request, accusing versus routine
Reads as an accusation

"We'll need to see proof of the damage before we can process anything."

Reads as routine

"So sorry about that! Could you send a quick photo when you get a chance? It helps me flag the batch to our supplier so it doesn't happen again — I'll get your refund started as soon as I see it."

Notice the second version also tells the customer what happens next and why the photo matters beyond just proving their claim — it becomes useful information for the brand, not a hoop to jump through.

Keep this
  • Frame verification as routine process, never as doubt about this specific person.
  • Give a reason the photo or detail helps beyond just proving the claim.
  • Tell the customer what happens once you have it — verification with no clear next step feels like stalling.
Do this now — 6 minutes

Rewrite an accusatory-sounding verification request into a routine-sounding one, using the worked example's shape.

Lesson 5.313 minutes

Where discretion lives: what you decide, what you escalate

Not every judgement call is yours to make alone, and knowing the line matters as much as having good judgement in the first place. A rep who escalates everything is nearly useless; a rep who never escalates anything eventually gives away money, stock, or trust the brand didn't authorise.

Three tiers, sorted by reversibility and stakes together

  1. Decide yourself. Squarely within policy, low value, a pattern you've handled before with a known-good outcome.
  2. Ask first, briefly, with a recommendation. A genuine grey area or a value above your comfort threshold, but not urgent enough that a short delay causes harm.
  3. Escalate immediately. A policy gap with real money attached, anything that could become a legal or safety issue, or a customer explicitly asking for someone more senior.

The same request can sit in different tiers depending on the brand and the amount — a ₦5,000 goodwill discount might be squarely tier one at Bumi Naturals, and tier two the moment it's ₦50,000. Know your own thresholds, in writing, rather than guessing them fresh each time.

When genuinely unsure, treat it as the higher tier

Asking unnecessarily costs a founder a few minutes. Guessing wrong on something with real money or reputation attached costs considerably more — the same principle Course 01's escalation lesson applies to a virtual assistant's work.

Keep this
  • Three tiers: decide alone, ask with a recommendation, escalate immediately.
  • The same request can shift tiers by value — know your own thresholds in writing.
  • When unsure, treat it as the higher tier. The cost of asking is small; the cost of guessing wrong on something serious isn't.
Do this now — 7 minutes

Sort five real or made-up refund requests into the three tiers, and write your reasoning for each.

Lesson 5.412 minutes

Goodwill gestures: when they retain a customer, and when they train one to complain

A free gift, a discount, an extra item thrown in — goodwill gestures genuinely work to keep a frustrated customer. They also, used carelessly, teach a customer that complaining is the fastest way to get something free, which creates more complaints rather than fewer.

The test that tells the two apart

A goodwill gesture answering a real, verified problem builds loyalty. A gesture handed out because a customer sounded annoyed, with nothing actually wrong, teaches the wrong lesson. The distinction isn't about being generous or stingy — it's whether the gesture is responding to a genuine issue or to volume of complaint alone.

Two goodwill calls, same brand, different outcomes
Builds loyalty

A verified late delivery, first time for this customer. A small discount on their next order, offered without being asked, alongside the apology. Reads as generous, unprompted, and tied to something real.

Trains the wrong behaviour

A customer complains loudly that a product "isn't as described" with no specifics, and immediately asks what compensation they'll get. A free item handed over here, with nothing actually verified, is noticed — and repeated next time.

Keep this
  • A gesture answering a verified problem builds loyalty; one answering volume of complaint trains more complaints.
  • Offering a gesture unprompted, tied to something real, reads as more generous than one that was extracted.
  • The question is never "generous or stingy" — it's "is this responding to a real issue."
Do this now — 5 minutes

Write one situation where a goodwill gesture is clearly earned, and one where it clearly isn't, using the same brand for both.

Lesson 5.510 minutes

Documenting the exception so the next rep isn't stuck explaining it

An exception granted and never written down doesn't stay an exception for long. The next rep sees the order history, notices a refund was approved outside policy, and — reasonably, with no other information — assumes that's now how it's done. Module 5's whole discipline collapses one undocumented exception at a time.

What belongs in an exception log

The specific clause or window that was set aside, the reason, and — critically — that it was a one-time exception rather than a new rule. Two lines is usually enough.

A logged exception

"Order #2311 — refunded a sale item at day 40 (outside the 30-day window and normally final-sale). Exception: 3-year repeat customer, documented family emergency delayed the return. One-time — does not set a new policy."

Keep this
  • Log the clause set aside, the reason, and that it was one-time — not a new rule.
  • Two lines is usually enough; the point is a record existing at all.
  • An unlogged exception becomes an accidental precedent the moment another rep sees it.
Do this now — 5 minutes

Write a log entry for the exception you granted in 5.3's drill or 5.4's worked example, in the shape above.

Module 5 drillfifty-five minutes

Ten borderline refund requests

Ten refund and return requests are supplied, checked against Bumi Naturals' actual return policy. Some are clearly within policy, some clearly outside it, and several sit right on an edge deliberately.

Deliver three things per request

  1. The decision — approve, decline, or a documented exception, with the specific clause it turns on.
  2. The reply — the actual message you'd send, using 5.2's verification shape where a check is needed.
  3. The tier — decide-yourself, ask-first, or escalate, using 5.3's key, with a one-line reason.

How it is marked

  • Is the policy read correctly? The most common failure is answering from a remembered gist rather than the actual clause — 5.1's whole point.
  • Does a verification request read as routine rather than accusatory? A reply that sounds suspicious fails 5.2 even if the underlying decision is right.
  • Is the tier defensible? Not whether it matches an answer key exactly — whether the reasoning holds under 5.3's test.
  • Is every exception logged in the shape from 5.5? An undocumented exception fails this drill regardless of whether the underlying call was good.
What this drill is really testing

Whether policy, verification, escalation judgement and documentation all show up together on the same request — not any one of them in isolation.
